How to think different

Your 1st step to breakthrough writing with AI

Author: Nicolas Cole
Date: 2025-11-07

[

](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FrHf!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F27035766-4e37-4d7c-bd95-0b7363f162e8_2000x1428.png)

The single fastest way to increase your value (and earnings) as a writer is to provide differentiated thinking.

I built a 7-figure career working with startup founders, company executives, and investors on refining their thinking and publishing their unique insights at scale on the Internet (and it’s why I can charge 100x+ more than the “ghostwriters” you find on Upwork.com).

But in the age of AI, thinking feels inefficient.

When a robot can run circles around you, it’s only logical to think delegating your work to a machine is how to stay ahead of the curve.

The problem is “AI writing” is an easily replaceable commodity.

However, if you start thinking (not just writing words), but unique, unheard of, unidentified perspectives on your topic, suddenly you become a luxury good that people will line up to buy with their time, attention, and even their money.

Real value comes from the interpretation of the world, not the transcription of it. And the writers who win in the next decade aren’t the ones who write more words—they’re the ones who bring more original thought to those words. Which means if you want to increase your market value as a writer, you need to stop asking: “What should I write today?” and start asking: “What’s missing?”

That one question can change your career.

So, how do you think different?

It starts with recognizing and reframing your immediate reaction to a topic.

Read the following words (slowly) and just notice what happens in your brain.

  • Guns

  • Abortion

  • Flat earth

  • Billionaires

  • Democrats

  • Republicans

  • Free-speech

  • Climate change

What did you notice?

Did you have any immediate reactions? Do you already have an opinion?

The “Thinking Game”

You are responsible for changing the way your reader “thinks.”

Said differently, good writing transforms your reader. And you are successful when you’ve moved their thinking from the old way to the new and different way you are educating them about.

But what is “thinking?”

According to Roger Martin, arguably the world’s #1 management thinker, “thinking” is when you look at the world through an existing model. It’s how you use learnings from the past to make sense of the present. So when another driver cuts you off on the highway, you instantly apply your past experiences to the present and swerve to avoid an accident.

Your reflex saves your life.

Almost all thinking is “reflexive” rather than “reflective.”

Consider the difference:

  • “Reflexive” thinking: Having an unconscious “reflex” in response to ideas or opinions.

  • “Reflective” thinking: Taking a moment to consciously reflect on how the past may have created a preexisting mental model keeping you from considering a new and different future.

Reflexive thinking causes a scarcity of fresh thinking in the world because it relies on mental scaffolding built in the past.

Some of the smartest people stopped reflective thinking a long time ago. I would even go so far as to say that being declared a smart person is almost certain to make you stupid. Because when you get called “smart,” you become entrenched in your comfortable past. When you’re smart, you know things. And most people who know things are called “experts.” Which means they already know. And when you already know, by definition you are using old mental scaffolding to consider new and different futures.

Which makes you stupid.

So, don’t strive to become an expert (ever!) — it’s the enemy of fresh thinking.

[

](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!NZQ2!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa72b7fb9-6de4-420c-8bfa-e0453cd9cc8e_1442x1050.png)

To create fresh ideas, you have to know the difference between Obvious & Non-Obvious Content.

And here’s why:

  • Obvious Content: The art of speaking to what people already think and believe to be true (catering to the reader’s reflexive nature).

  • Non-Obvious Content: The art of educating people on what they haven’t thought about or decided they believe yet (requesting their reflective nature).

It’s crucial to understand which of these two consumption states you are creating for, and where you are “meeting the reader” — long before you write even a single word.

Because if you try feeding Non-Obvious content (that requires reflection and challenging one’s own mental models) to someone in an Obvious (reflexive) state, you will fail to get their attention and/or they’ll likely become frustrated at your inability to cater to their preconceived notions. And conversely, if you try feeding Obvious content to someone starving for Non-Obvious insights, you will burden them with boredom and/or they’ll likely become frustrated with your wasting their time, even insulting them (“This is so Obvious! Make me think!”).

  • Reflexive thinkers want Obvious content.

  • Reflective thinkers want Non-Obvious content.

Becoming a reflective thinker who knows who you are creating for, and what their expectations are (and why), is half the battle to becoming a legendary writer.

The FOR WHO / SO THAT framework

Most writers start with what they want to say instead of thinking about their reader first.

This is a mistake. And it’s why AI is so good at producing generic content. When Claude or ChatGPT doesn’t know who you are writing for or why, it makes a bazillion assumptions that net out to the most generic “reflexive” version of your topic.

But when you get specific about WHO you’re writing for SO THAT they can do WHAT, you are forced to think start thinking different. You become conscious of what you are using to evaluate your topic (which “lens” you are looking through).

And then before you react, respond, or give in to your reflexive nature, you pause and first consider which mental model you’re using to examine the information being presented. You train yourself to be curious, to ask why, to suspend your past opinions, beliefs, and mental models, and to open the aperture of your mind and consider something different.

That’s “thinking.”

And it starts with two simple questions:

WHO am I writing for?

SO THAT they can do… what?

Let me show you how this works.

Example:

If you sat down to write about “How to use AI for writing” without getting specific, you’d get what you expect:

  • Generic tips anyone could give

  • Obvious advice everyone’s already heard

  • Surface-level content that doesn’t help anyone

But watch what happens when you use the FOR WHO / SO THAT framework:

Version 1:

  • FOR WHO: First-time AI users who’ve never opened ChatGPT

  • SO THAT: They can write their first email without staring at a blank screen

Now you’re forced to think reflectively:

  • What do first-timers NOT know that everyone assumes they do?

  • What overwhelms them that experienced users forget about?

  • What’s the FIRST thing they need, not the tenth?

You’d write: “Open ChatGPT. Type this exact prompt. Here’s what to look for in the output. Here’s how to tell if it’s good or needs another pass.”

Compare that to version 2.

Version 2:

  • FOR WHO: Experienced writers who’ve been using AI for months but keep getting bland outputs

  • SO THAT: They can create content that sounds like them, not ChatGPT

Again you’re forced into reflective thinking:

  • What are experienced users doing WRONG that produces bland content?

  • What assumption are they making that they need to challenge?

  • What do they need to UNLEARN?

You’d write about bringing YOUR thinking first, using AI as a junior staff writer, not a replacement for your voice.

See the difference?

Same topic. Completely different content. Because getting more specific about your audience forced you into more reflective thinking.

How to get specific with FOR WHO / SO THAT

Now, when you’re deciding WHO you’re writing for, there are a few ways to get specific:

  • Experience Level: Beginner vs. Intermediate vs. Advanced

  • Budget: No-budget person vs. Premium option seeker

  • Outcome: Make more money vs. Save time vs. Avoid mistakes

  • Context: Specific industry, location, or situation

  • Sub-niche: Not just “marketing” but “cold email for B2B SaaS”

The more specific you get with WHO and SO THAT, the more you activate reflective thinking—because you can’t be generic anymore.

The cool thing is you can train ChatGPT to help you.

“But Cole… if I use AI, isn’t that just reflexive thinking?”

No.

Because the point isn’t to let AI choose an audience for you. The point is: you use AI to expose the edges of the space you could choose from. But you choose. AI is just a grip strength tool here.

You still have to look at the options and decide:

  • Which one is actually interesting

  • Which one you have something to say about

  • Which one aligns with your lived experience

  • Which one has the highest potential tension

AI can spit out 7 possible FOR WHOs.

But you still must reflect on: “Which of these is the most interesting to pursue? And why?” That decision is the reflective thinking. The AI step is just speed.

That’s it!

Now you know the difference between reflexive and reflective thinking. And you know how to use “for who / so that” to activate the reflective mode.
