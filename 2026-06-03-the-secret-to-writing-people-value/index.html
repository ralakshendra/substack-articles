The single most common mistake I see writers make is so subtle that most of them never catch it—even after years of writing.

They use intangible language.

Let me show you exactly what I mean, and why it’s quietly costing you readers.

The Gut-Reaction Test

Quick gut reaction.

Which of these do you value more?

  • “Here’s an explanation of how I improved my Facebook ads by 50%.”

  • “The Facebook Ads Blueprint: A Proven 5-Step System For Increasing Conversions By 50%”

One more:

  • “Why I really don’t like dating in my 50s — and what I’d recommend if you want to find a trustworthy partner later in life.”

  • “The Later-In-Life Lovebook: The 5 Mistakes You’re Making When Dating In Your 50s (And How To Fix Them)”

I’ve run this little exercise with a lot of people. And it doesn’t matter who they are. Ask 10 of them, and all 10 pick the second option. Every time. They want the Blueprint. They want the Lovebook.

The question is… why?

Here’s what’s going on under the hood.

Intangibles are ideas.

  • “Here’s what I have to say on this topic.”

  • “Here’s how to be happy and live a long life.”

  • “Here’s my explanation of how to be successful.”

It’s subtle, but notice how all of those are intangible. You can’t hold them in your hands. There’s nothing physical to grab onto. And because of that, they’re hard to value — because now the burden is on the reader to figure out how valuable your idea even is.

Tangibles are objects (or at least give the perception of being objects).

  • Template

  • Playbook

  • Workbook

  • Spreadsheet

  • Email Course

  • Ultimate Guide

Why do those words feel more valuable? Because your brain treats them like things it could pick up and put on a shelf. Things it could own.

Which gives us the rule:

Intangibles are vague. Tangibles are specific.

But this goes way past slapping the word “Blueprint” on something and calling it a day.

Watch how much harder a headline hits the more tangible we make it:

  • [V1] Unlock Passive Income!

  • [V2] The Passive Income Blueprint: How To Get Rich & Be Happy

  • [V3] The Passive Short-Term Rental Empire Blueprint: How To Get Rich From Real Estate & Be Happy

  • [V4] The Passive Short-Term Rental Empire Blueprint: How To Land Your 1st Deal, Maintain A Steady Stream Of Tenants, And Build A Portfolio Of Properties Generating $100,000 Per Month

  • [V5] The Passive Short-Term Rental Empire Blueprint: How To Land Your 1st Deal, Maintain A Steady Stream Of Tenants, And Build A Portfolio Of Properties Generating $100,000 Per Month (Even If You’ve Never Bought Real Estate & Are Terrified Of Losing Money!)

It should be obvious that V5 hits exponentially harder than V1.

The whole game — the thing I want you to get good at — is learning how to skip straight to V5 from the blank page.

The answer is a skill I’ve become obsessed by—unbundling.

Unbundling Words, Operationalizing Behavior

This is one of my favorite Alex Hormozi-isms: unbundling words and operationalizing behavior.

Here’s what it looks like in practice. Inside his companies — and the companies they invest in — they don’t train salespeople by telling them to “be more confident on calls.” They hand them a checklist of the actual behaviors a good call is made of. And they get absurdly granular about it. Things like:

  • Did you greet the prospect by name, with energy in your voice?

  • Did you nod while they were talking?

  • Did you repeat the last thing they said back to them to show you understood?

That might sound ridiculous. It’s not. That’s exactly the level of detail a beginner needs.

Because here’s the alternative: you tell someone “be more confident,” and… nobody knows what that means. “Confident” is a bundled word. It’s a folder. And until you unbundle it — crack it open and pull out the specific actions inside — nobody can understand it, and more importantly, nobody can do it.

No unbundling, no change in behavior.

Say a writer sits down to write about confidence. They write 800 clean, correct words about confidence. Every sentence is true. And the reader gets to the end and thinks… yeah, looks good.

Looks good. Not “this is exactly what I needed.” Not “how did you get inside my head?” Just… looks good.

Because the writer handed the reader the word “confidence” back, all dressed up in 800 words. And the reader already had the word. What they didn’t have was the stuff inside it — and that’s the only part they came for.

This is the part nobody tells you when you’re starting out:

You can be 100% right and still be 100% useless.

How To Unbundle Your Writing To Make It More Tangible

The move itself is one question you ask before you write a single sentence:

“Okay — but what does a person actually DO?”

You take the bundled word — confidence, discipline, “good copy,” “passive income” — and you refuse to write until you’ve pulled it apart into things a person actually does. Real actions. Stuff a camera could film. You don’t stop at “listen well,” because “listen well” is still a folder. You keep going until it’s “ask one follow-up question before you add your own point.”

Then:

  1. Unbundle wide. List every action and every specific inside the concept. Push past the obvious first three — they’re usually still bundled.

  2. Cut hard. You’ll have 12-15. A good piece needs 3-7. Keep the most surprising, the most ignored, the most important. This is where the real writing happens: in deciding what stays.

  3. Sequence. Put the survivors in the order a reader needs them. First thing first.

  4. Write. Your headers already exist — they’re your actions. Now you’re just filling in the tactical how-to.

Let’s unbundle the word “unbundle” itself.

Concept word: Unbundle

The question: What does a writer actually DO when they unbundle a topic?

First pass, unfiltered:

  • Writes the concept word at the top of a blank doc

  • Writes underneath: “What does someone DO when they ___?”

  • Answers it once, writes it down

  • Asks “what else?” and writes that too

  • Keeps going without stopping to judge anything

  • Runs out of obvious answers

  • Asks “what would a beginner skip?” and adds those

  • Reads the list back

  • Asks “could a camera film this?” for each item

  • Circles the ones that still feel abstract

  • Asks the question again on the circled items

  • Writes more specific versions, crosses out the abstract originals

  • Counts what’s left

  • Asks “which of these does my reader actually need?”

  • Crosses out the rest

  • Reads the remaining items in order

  • Reorders anything that feels out of sequence

Seventeen items. Several of them describe the same phase. “Answers it once, writes it down / asks what else / keeps going without stopping to judge” are all one action: list everything fast without filtering. “Circles the abstract ones / asks the question again / writes more specific versions / crosses out the originals” are all one action: refine anything still vague. Collapse them.

After the cut:

  1. Write the concept word and the question: “What does someone DO when they ___?”

  2. List everything fast, without filtering, until nothing comes

  3. Push past the obvious: ask “what would a beginner skip?”

  4. Read back the list; ask “could a camera film this?” Cross out anything that can’t

  5. Cut to the 3-7 most important

  6. Sequence them in the order a reader needs

Six steps.

Notice anything? That’s the “How To Do It” section of this piece. The whole newsletter is “unbundle” unbundled.

When To Unbundle Your Writing

So when do you actually do this?

The instinct is to treat unbundling like a finishing move — something you sprinkle on at the end to make a headline pop. That’s backwards. The real wins come from running it at specific moments throughout your process, and most of those moments come earlier than you’d think.

Here’s where it matters most:

1. Before you write: start with the title.

Your title is a bundled concept. “How to build trust with your reader.” “The discipline of great writers.” “What good copy actually is.”

Sit with that title before you open a doc. Ask the question: what does a person actually do when they have this thing? The answers become your sections. The piece writes itself from there.

This is why some outlines take five minutes and others take two hours. Writers who skip this step have to discover their sections by writing. Writers who unbundle first already know where they’re going.

2. Your draft feels hollow or preachy.

You finish something and it’s technically correct but somehow useless. You’re telling readers to do something without telling them how. That’s a bundled draft.

Go back to the concept at the center of the piece and unbundle it. The hollow sections will become obvious immediately.

3. You get a bundled critique.

“This piece lacks authority.” “Your writing feels flat.” “You need a stronger voice.”

These sound like feedback. They’re labels. The writer doesn’t know what to edit because the critique doesn’t describe any action.

Unbundle the critique the same way you’d unbundle a topic. What does a person actually do when their writing has authority? What observable thing makes a piece feel strong instead of flat? Now you have a checklist you can edit against.

4. You’re teaching something you do intuitively.

Good writers do this without knowing what they’re doing.

If you’re building a course, writing a framework, or coaching students, you need to drag the invisible mechanics into the open. Felt sense doesn’t teach. Unbundling translates it into steps someone else can follow.

(This is also true when writing prompts for AI. “Write something persuasive” is a bundled instruction. Unbundle persuasion first and your prompt gets 10x more specific, which means the output gets 10x more useful.)

Which brings me to something we built for you.

The Unbundle Prompt

Doing this by hand gets easy with reps — but the first few times, it’s slow and a little awkward.

So we made a prompt that does the heavy lifting. You hand it any bundled word, and it cracks the thing wide open into concrete, trainable actions in about ten seconds. Run enough concepts through it and you’ll start unbundling in your head on autopilot, which is the whole point. The prompt just gets you there faster.

Drop it into Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini — wherever you work.

Replace “Term” with whatever bundled thing you want to crack open. Charisma. “Be more strategic.” A workflow that’s been a mess for months.

Here’s the prompt:

XYZ is a "bundled" term. Assume it houses a lot of connotative and intangible meaning that is hard to train and understand. Your mission is to "Unbundle" and operationalize this term.

Operationalizing means to turn the phrase or word into a set of trainable and learnable and visible actions that when mastered, encompass the real meaning of the term.

For example: "Charisma" is a bundled term that stands for the feeling of warmth, power, and presence other people feel when they interact with you. The actions that it encircles include making eye contact, active listening, speaking slowly, asking questions about the other person, smiling genuinely, having a firm handshake, and setting a collaborative frame for the conversation, etc.

Provide CONCRETE examples for daily activities in work or life, business or relationships, interpersonal or mindset, in a way that someone could take action on without necessarily feeling the emotion that might naturally lead to the behavior.

TERM: 
[INSERT A BUNDLED TERM OR PHRASE]

Tip — start with the word that’s been nodded at but never acted on. That’s the most-bundled term in your head right now.

The Payoff

Here’s the part I care about most. (It’s why I still do this, even now — 10 years and a few thousand articles later.)

When you sit down to unbundle a concept, one of two things happens.

Either you produce a real list of specific, tangible actions — in which case your piece is basically pre-outlined, your headline writes itself, and you go fast.

Or… you can’t. All you can produce is more fuzzy words. “Passive income means making money while you sleep. Earning without working.” Which is just the same vague idea wearing a fake mustache.

That second outcome isn’t a failure. It’s the move doing you a favor — telling you something you needed to hear: you don’t understand this topic well enough to write about it yet.

And learning that now, in five minutes at the topic stage, beats learning it from your readers three hours into a draft. (Or worse — your readers don’t tell you anything. They just quietly stop opening you.)

So before you write your next big, fuzzy, important-sounding topic, do this first:

Ask, “What does a person actually DO?”

Write the list.

Look at it.

Real list of actions → you’ve got your next piece (and your V5 headline). Just fog → you’ve got your next thing to learn.

Either way, you didn’t waste a draft.
