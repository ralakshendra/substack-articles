    Writer’s block is a choice problem.

    When you sit down to write and feel stuck, what’s actually happening?

    You’re thinking, “I don’t know what choice to make in this moment. I don’t know if the story I’m telling should go down this tangent or this tangent.”

    You have options in front of you—you just don’t want to pick one.

    So you sit there waiting for the “right” answer to appear, like there’s some objectively correct choice hiding behind the cursor.

    There isn’t.

    The irony of writer’s block is it’s the decision to do the only thing that is truly the wrong answer—pick nothing at all.

    You would be way better off just picking something.

    Because the choice of something is always better than choosing to do nothing.

    So, when you feel stuck, stop asking “what should I write?”

    And instead ask: “what choice am I avoiding?” — and then make it.

    Once you accept that writing is choice-making, the next question is: what makes a good choice?

    When I’m writing non-fiction, I’m always cycling through these in my head:

    • Should I give a tip?

    • Should I share a mistake?

    • Should I share a lesson learned?

    • Should I share a reason why the reader should do this?

    At the end of the day, these are just choices.

    But you have to have some baseline level of competence in order to sit there and go, okay, let me come up with a compelling choice and you have to have a reason for making that choice.

    Competence is the prerequisite that makes choice meaningful.

    If I’m operating from a place of competence, picking mistakes and sharing compelling mistakes is just as high-quality a choice as picking lessons and sharing high-quality lessons.

    They’re both right answers.

    That’s the whole game. Build competence so your options are good. Then make choices on purpose.

    What separates great writers from average ones isn’t that they have better ideas. It’s that they make sharper choices, more often, with more intention.

    Leverage AI to explore options instead of giving the robots free rein over your thinking.

    This is also where most writers go wrong with AI writing.

    They want Claude or ChatGPT to write for them. This is a mistake.

    When you completely defer all of the choices to AI, even if it produces something cool or even if it produces something great. Sure, you skipped to the end, but you literally just removed all of the fun.

    It's like buying a brand new video game as a kid and not even giving yourself the opportunity to play the game. You just install the Game Shark and instantly give yourself all the levels and all the items and what's the point?

    Why'd you even buy the game?

    The point of writing is making and expressing choices.

    When you ask AI to “write like you,” you’re asking it to make choices the same way you would, every time—which is impossible.

    But that doesn’t make AI useless.

    It just means you’ve been pointing it at the wrong job.

    Think of AI like a writers’ room. A place to iterate faster. A second brain that helps you see your draft from outside your own head.

    • It’s great for brainstorming.

    • It’s great for feeding in a draft and getting a first round of feedback before you bother a real human.

    • It’s great at presenting you with options you wouldn’t have thought of.

    What it’s not good for is making your choices for you. The moment you let it do that, you’ve stopped writing — you’re just publishing.

    So, what should you do when you’re stuck?

    Use AI to inspect the choices in front of you.

    Use it whenever you’re stuck—at any stage:

    • You have a topic but no angle.

    • You have an angle but no structure.

    • You don’t know what to write about.

    • You’re mid-draft and stuck on what comes next.

    • You have a finished draft and something feels off.

    Paste this into Claude or ChatGPT:

    You are my writing assistant, but with one strict rule: you do not write 
my draft for me. You only surface choices.

Here's what we're going to do:

I'll tell you where I'm stuck.

Then I want you to give me 3-5 different options to help me get unstuck.

For example:

- Different topics I could write about
- Different angles or arguments I could take
- Different ways to organize my writing
- Different directions the next section could go
- Different things that might be wrong (pacing, clarity, opening, ending, 
  tone, claims, etc.)

For each choice, write:
- A short label (3-6 words)
- A one-sentence description
- Where this choice leads
- What I gain and what I give up — the trade-offs of making this choice

Be honest. If a choice is safer but more boring, say so. If it's riskier 
but more memorable, say so. Don't hedge.

Now, one of these choices is probably the "obvious" one — the path most 
writers would take on autopilot. Tell me which one and why. I want to 
know what the safe, predictable answer looks like so I can decide whether 
to take it or break from it on purpose.

Important: The decision is mine. You're here to widen the options, not 
narrow them.

---

Now, ask me what I'm working on and where I'm stuck.

Help me describe in 1-3 sentences what I'm trying to do and what feels 
wrong or unfinished. If I don't know what's wrong, help me handle the 
diagnosis.

Common examples of where I might be stuck:

- I don't know what to write about
- I have a topic, but don't know what to say about it
- I have an angle, but don't know how to organize it
- I'm mid-piece and stuck on what comes next
- I have a draft, but something feels off

Gather any context I already have (audience, idea, topic, what I'm 
trying to write, etc.).

Ask me one question at a time. Let me answer before asking the next one. 
Be helpful and push my thinking.

Understand?

    Notice what happen after you run the prompt.

    You’ll have a reaction.

    • “Yes! This one.”

    • “This one, plus something else.”

    • “Terrible. Wrong direction entirely, I want something else.”

    When AI puts options in front of you, you don't get to wait for the "right" one to appear.

    Making the choice forces you to think. You have to decide what you actually want to say. And every time you decide with intention — not just by defaulting to the first thing — you sharpen the judgment that makes the next decision faster and more yours.

    The choice is the point.
