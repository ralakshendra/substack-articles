    In the early 1900s, Claude Hopkins, one of the early pioneers of advertising, had a BIG idea.

    Before toothpaste had a tingling or minty sensation, brushing your teeth didn’t feel particularly satisfying. Even though the mechanical act of brushing removed plaque and helped oral health, there was no immediate sensory feedback to signal that your mouth was clean.

    That is, until Pepsodent, a toothpaste brand, launched in the 1910s.

    Claude Hopkins helped market it. And his BIG idea was that if they added a cooling, minty ingredient into the product (specifically citric acid and later menthol/eucalyptus) that caused a tingling sensation, customers would FEEL the “result” of toothpaste significantly more.

    And he was right.

    That tingling sensation didn’t actually clean your teeth any better.

    And yet, before Pepsodent… toothbrushing was rare.

    And after Pepsodent… it became a daily ritual for millions of Americans.

    The BIG Idea Matters Most

    The best copy in the world doesn’t really “convince” people of anything.

    All it does it make them aware of something they weren’t aware of before. Sometimes, that means changing the product slightly. Sometimes, not. But irresistible copy isn’t about convincing. It’s about setting the stage so they convince themselves.

    And after generating millions and millions of dollars myself—both selling our own products/programs/services, but also copywriting for other brands, publicly traded companies, and Silicon Valley startup—I’ve assembled a few frameworks that I think would be helpful for you.

    Here’s how to find BIG ideas (and the AI prompt to get you started):

    Framework #1: Think In Opposites

    One of the easiest ways to immediately differentiate a product or service is do this simple exercise:

    • Make a list of all the things that are “conventional wisdom” in an existing industry or niche.

    • Make a list of all the benefits of that existing industry or niche.

    • And make a list of all the problems in that existing industry or niche.

    …then, find ways to say the opposite.

    For example, here’s how we did this within our beginner writing program, Ship 30 for 30. First, we remind everyone how the writing world is “today.” (Aka: conventional wisdom.) Then, we just say the opposite—which immediately presents the benefits of our approach as being “different.”

    [

    ](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!wCuC!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7e00cb49-bb51-4dcf-87f7-60074de17bee_1200x630.png)

    That, in itself, is a BIG idea.

    • Take what everyone currently believes about an industry/niche.

    • Find ways to say the opposite.

    Very simple!

    Framework #2: Turn an industry’s biggest problem into its biggest benefit.

    Let’s take collectable trading cards (like Pokémon) as an example.

    • Biggest Problem = Trading cards are printed on cheap paper, easy to lose, easy to damage, etc.

    • New Biggest Benefit = But (BIG idea here) if we give these trading cards a “grade” based on quality, and then put them inside a protective case, suddenly they become extremely rare & valuable collectables.

    [

    ](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!thtJ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fda1cdac3-488e-4c3f-9c79-17763731d5fc_1440x1440.jpeg)

    Most of the time, a BIG idea is hiding in plain sight.

    And it’s really just a matter of taking an industry or product’s biggest problem and finding a way to shine a spotlight on it and make it “its biggest benefit.”

    Framework #3: Invent a problem your product or service solves.

    This one is a bit more advanced, but here’s a crazy example to show you how this works (and how powerful this can be).

    This is how Purdue Pharma differentiated their highly addictive painkiller, OxyContin, in the late 1990s.

    To separate OxyContin from other painkillers, Purdue Pharma created a new term in their marketing materials—and invented a problem their product could solve. Instead of saying OxyContin relieved “pain” (which is what every pharmaceutical company said), they gave the specific type of pain OxyContin treated a name—a name they made up themselves. They said OxyContin relieved “breakthrough pain,” which is the pain you feel despite having taken a painkiller. Your pain “breaks through,” regardless.

    And what customer wants to experience “breakthrough pain?”

    Nobody—exactly.

    It’s so subtle, and yet this one term led to billions of dollars of demand (and, sadly, hundreds of thousands of deaths)

    Another example:

    People have always had bad breath—but didn’t think of it as a social disease. Listerine coined the term halitosis, gave it clinical weight, and made it embarrassing. Sales exploded from $100K to $4M in 7 years.

    Why You Should Start With The BIG Idea

    Almost every great marketing campaign, ever, can be traced back to a very simple BIG idea.

    And listen, you might have no interest in becoming a “marketing executive.” That’s fine. I don’t either. But in order to write effective copy, this is where it all starts. Because your copy has to SAY SOMETHING DIFFERENT. And the way you say something different all comes down to the BIG Idea.

    So, before you:

    • Write a subject line

    • Write an email

    • Write an ad

    • Write anything

    …you need to spend some time getting to know the customer, familiarizing yourself with the product or service you are “selling,” and then doing some good old-fashioned thinking about some potential BIG Ideas.
