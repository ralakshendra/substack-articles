    5-Steps Visualize An Idea With Claude

    Here's the thing about visualizing ideas:

    While there are endless ways to draw something, for educational content, you only need four types of visuals:

    • Why (The Ladder) - When you're showing progress

    • How (The Circle) - When you're showing connections

    • What (The Box/Grid) - When you're showing steps

    • What If (The Split Screen) - When you're showing transformation

    [

    ](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lRT4!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbee69687-8a29-43ab-a3d5-32ee09c2b688_1273x837.png)

    Before you draw anything, you need to think about your idea and the best way to "show it."

    Which brings us to the first step in the framework visualization process.

    Step 1: Ask yourself: "What am I really trying to communicate?"

    The way you choose a visual style is simple:

    Look for the patterns in your idea.

    If your idea is about:

    • Progress or increasing value → Use the Ladder

    • How things work together → Use Circles

    • Clear steps or categories → Use a Grid

    • Before/After change → Use Split Screen

    For example, if you're explaining "why reading matters," you'd use a Ladder to show the increasing value. But if you're showing "how reading affects your brain," you'd use Circles to show the connections between reading, learning, and memory.

    [

    ](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!arBR!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5c2fff34-fbc3-43b0-b260-0a5de8fe4f75_1292x632.png)

    Step 2: Sketch the basic structure

    After you’ve selected a visual model, the next step is to draw the basic shapes in your visual.

    Let's say you want to visualize the idea: "Building a reading habit." Building a habit implies progress (going from non-reader to habitual reader), so you’d pick the WHY model.

    Here's what the initial sketch looks like:

    [

    ](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!hVHz!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb66e95e0-7f12-4eaa-b980-4d832a33946a_782x692.png)

    The first-draft results in four equal-sized boxes, stacked on top of each other—and simple labels for each level:

    • Non-Reader: "I don't have time"

    • Occasional Reader: "When I remember"

    • Regular Reader: "Part of my routine"

    • Habitual Reader: "Can't not read"

    That's it. Just boxes and labels in a ladder structure.

    Fight the urge to make it fancy. Just get the basic structure right first.

    Step 3: Layer in ONE or TWO visual decorations.

    [

    ](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-6Of!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcbb3083a-55da-40d8-a1fa-ce00f3ab5c01_1680x1182.png)

    Now that you’ve drawn your model, the next step is to decorate it.

    Like the visual model, you also have four different ways to make an idea "pop":

    • Symbols - Simple icons that everyone knows (✓, ↑, ♦, ⚡)

    • Scale - Making important things bigger or bolder

    • Metaphor - Using familiar concepts (like sunrise, journey, bridge)

    • Comparison - Showing contrast or progression

    Your job is to pick ONE or TWO ways to improve your basic drawing. Not all of them.

    Here's why:

    Your brain is like a busy coffee shop customer. Give them a simple menu with just a few great options, they'll make a choice and be happy. Hand them a 10-page menu with hundreds of drinks? They'll freeze up, get overwhelmed, and probably just order a plain coffee.

    The same thing happens with visuals.

    Every decoration you add is like adding another item to the menu. It forces your reader's brain to process one more thing:

    • "What does this symbol mean?"

    • "Why is this part bigger?"

    • "What's this color trying to tell me?"

    • "How do these things compare?"

    When you use all four decorations, you're basically yelling four different messages at once. Your reader won't know where to look or what to focus on.

    Let me show you how this transformed the visual we created in Step 2.

    [

    ](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!afZZ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F72f76332-008d-4932-99a0-5952dc5288ff_1344x698.png)

    See how just two visual elements transform the basic structure?

    1. The sunrise colors create a natural feeling of progress

    2. The increasing number of book icons reinforces the growing commitment to reading

    We didn't need to add:

    • Different box sizes (scale)

    • Arrows between boxes (comparison)

    • Any other decorations

    Simple. Clear. Memorable.

    Step 4: Give your visual the first glance test.

    Step away from your picture for 5 minutes. Then come back and look at it with fresh eyes. Ask yourself: "Is this what I really want to say?"

    Let's look at our reading habit diagram again:

    [

    ](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Iusu!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8611a171-4870-4402-bed5-4ffa0ac2589e_1208x1014.png)

    Our first version shows a nice progression from non-reader to habitual reader. Pretty colors. Clear stages. But wait...

    It's missing something crucial: the HOW.

    • How do you move from "I don't have time" to "When I remember"?

    • What makes someone shift from occasional to regular reading?

    • What's the actual mechanism of habit formation?

    It’s missing the "Skinny To Shredded" factor.

    Like a before/after fitness photo. When you see a transformation, you immediately think "How?" and click to learn more. That's what we need in this diagram.

    So we made a few modifications:

    1. Removed the middle stages to focus on the transformation

    2. Made the contrast specific and measurable (1 book → 50+ books per year)

    3. Added a how ("The Micro-Pages Method")

    4. Included just enough steps to make it feel attainable but leave them wanting more

    The result? A visual that makes you think: "Wait... they went from struggling with one book to reading 50+ on auto-pilot? And they did it with just 3 pages a day? How exactly does that work?"

    Sometimes showing less of the process opens a loop and creates curiosity.

    Now we’re getting somewhere.

    Two Prompt Visualization Process

    Creating visual frameworks with Claude is a two-step process.

    You'll use two different prompts in sequence.

    Prompt 1: Design The Basic Visual Structure

    The first prompt helps Claude choose and create the right type of visual structure for your concept.

    Here's how to use it:

    1. Copy the "Pick Your Structure" prompt provided below.

    2. Replace [INSERT YOUR CONCEPT] with your idea.

    3. Paste the complete prompt into your chat with Claude.

    Claude will analyze your concept and select the most appropriate framework type.

    Here’s the Pick Your Structure prompt:

    I want to visualize this concept: [INSERT YOUR CONCEPT]

Step 1: Model Selection Analysis

Please analyze the provided concept and determine which of these model types would be most effective:
- WHY Model (Ladder) - Best for showing value progression, motivation, or improvement journeys
- HOW Model (Venn) - Best for showing overlapping relationships or strategic overview
- WHAT Model (3x3 Matrix) - Best for showing tactical steps, processes, or classifications
- IF Model (Contrast Frame) - Best for showing transformations or before/after states

Consider:
- Is this about motivation/value? (Choose WHY)
- Is this about relationships/strategy? (Choose HOW)
- Is this about steps/tactics? (Choose WHAT)
- Is this about transformation? (Choose IF)

Step 2: Illustration Generation

Based on the selected model type, create an illustration following these specifications:

For WHY Model (Ladder):
- Create vertical progression
- Show clear progression markers
- Label current state and aspiration
- Add relevant value indicators (time/money/status/happiness)

For HOW Model (Venn):
- Label main concepts clearly
- Highlight any intersection points
- Include key relationships
- Add strategic action points

For WHAT Model (Matrix):
- Label rows and columns
- Number sequence of steps
- Include plus/minus indicators
- Show clear pathways

For IF Model (Contrast):
- Show clear before/after states
- Include gradient between states
- Add evolution indicators
- Highlight key transformations

GLOBAL DESIGN SPECIFICATIONS:
Colors:
- Use complementary color scheme
- Main elements: Solid colors
- Backgrounds: Subtle gradients
- Text: High contrast

Typography:
- Headers: Bold sans-serif
- Body text: Regular sans-serif
- Max 2 font families
- Clear hierarchy (3 sizes maximum)

Space:
- 20% minimum white space
- Consistent margins
- Clear visual hierarchy
- Room for labels to breathe

OUTPUT FORMAT:
1. Recommended model type with rationale
2. Complete visual structure specifications
3. Exact text placement guidance
4. Color and typography recommendations
5. Special considerations for AI rendering

    Prompt 2: Add Visual Power

    After Claude creates your initial framework, use the second prompt to enhance its visual appeal and clarity.

    This prompt helps Claude adjust the scale, emphasis, and contrast to your visual.

    Simply copy and paste the "Add Visual Power" prompt into the same chat window after receiving your initial framework.

    Here’s the Add Visual Power prompt:

    Now let's enhance this visual model by adding meaningful visual elements.

Review these options and suggest the best choice for my concept (only choose 1).

Symbols: Would simple icons/shapes help explain key points?
Scale: Should some elements be bigger/bolder to show importance?
Metaphor: Could a familiar concept (like sunrise, journey, bridge) strengthen the message?
Comparison: Would showing contrast or progression help?
Your visual changes should make the framework simpler and easier to understand. The goal is to communicate the idea in the simplest way possible to get the point across.

Please show me how the visual model looks with your suggested visual elements added, and explain why these specific elements strengthen the message.

    Try It Out!

    Take any process or system that works for you and try turning it into a visual framework.

    Literally anything you do in your life that you do on repeat—you can visualize.

    Think about:

    • Your morning routine

    • Your system for acquiring new skills

    • Etc.

    Plug your frameworks into the prompts above and start generating memorable images for your newsletter, your LinkedIn posts, or anywhere you share content online.

    And if you don't like what Claude creates the first time, just say "Can you try that again with [your specific adjustment]?" Claude is happy to iterate until you get exactly what you want.
