Hey there Digital Writers!

Who is reading your writing?

A human! (That’s right.)

Which means you want to think of everything you write as a “letter.” And just like a letter to a friend, you’d open with something like “Hey there!” Something light & casual. And that tone should find its way throughout your writing.

Avoid the “professional” tone.

Just say it like it is. Break things down. Be honest. Friend to friend.

Shaan Puri, co-founder of the Milk Road and the My First Million podcast, is famous for bookending his writing with “Dear son” and “Love Dad.”

[

](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!hXBB!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F31533633-dda3-443f-8e54-bee424eda463_1192x466.png)

Here’s what Shaan knows:

Once you know WHO you’re writing to, everything gets easier—especially your intro.

Why?

Because it helps you think of the person you are writing for. It immediately sets the tone and context.

  • "Hey there son"

  • "Hey there Digital Writer"

  • "Hey overworked startup founder"

  • "Hey ghostwriter trying to land your first client"

Writing to your son or daughter is going to have a very different tone, style, and focus than writing to a group of busy professionals or someone just starting out in their career.

When you're clear on who you're talking to, you can write directly to their needs, wants, questions, and objections, and as a result making the very next thing you write “your introduction” hyper-relevant and mouth-watering for your reader.

Now…

As far as “content” goes in your introduction, here’s a really easy template you can use to get readers immediately hooked—with examples filled in:

{1 Sentence Strong Opinion}

If people are opening your newseltter but not sticking around to read it, your intro isn’t doing its job.

{3-5 Sentences Elaborate On Problems}

The reason this happens is most writers start with a loooong, slow, generic opening. They either ramble, over-explain, or worse, never get to the point. Readers expect more, especially with so much content competing for attention. A weak intro means lost readers—even if you’ve written an epic in-depth, valuable newsletter. The key is knowing how to pull your reader in line-by-line, quickly leading them to care about what’s coming next.

{1 Sentence Transition To Solutions}

The good news? Fixing this is easy.

{3-5 Sentences On “What We’re Going To Talk About Today”}

Today, we’re going to cover 10 simple ways to start your next newsletter. These are quick and effective strategies to keep people reading. And the cool thing is, with AI, you can try them all out fast until you find one that fits.

{Closing Sentence}

Let’s dive into the first strategy so you can start writing magnetic intros today.

If you scroll through my Medium library, or read any of our past newsletters, you’ll see this intro format all over the place.

But it’s not the only structure that works.

There’s a ton of them, and we are going look at 10 today.

10 Intro Recipes To Hook Your Reader

Here’s the list:

1. Question → Problem → Solution

  • Start with a thought-provoking question.

  • Identify the problem or challenge related to the question.

  • Introduce the solution or key topic you’ll be discussing.

Example:

"Why do so many newsletters

lose readers after the first few lines? It’s because most intros are too slow and don’t grab attention. In this post, you’ll learn how to hook readers immediately and keep them reading."

2. Anecdote → Relevance → Preview

  • Share a short personal or relatable story.

  • Explain why this story is relevant to the reader.

  • Offer a glimpse of what the reader will learn.

Example:

"Last month, I stared at a blank screen for two hours, trying to write the perfect intro. Turns out, I’m not alone—most writers struggle with this. But once I learned a simple formula, I started knocking out great intros in minutes. I’ll show you how."

3. Shocking Fact → Context → Hook

  • Start with a surprising or eye-catching fact or statistic.

  • Give background on why that fact is important.

  • Draw the reader into the topic and why they should care.

Example:

"Did you know that 90% of readers never make it past the first paragraph? That means your intro is make-or-break. In this post, I’ll show you exactly how to craft an intro that keeps people reading."

4. Bold Claim → Justification → Roadmap

  • Make a strong or bold statement to capture attention.

  • Support that claim with a brief explanation.

  • Outline what the reader will get from reading further.

Example:

"Anyone can write a killer intro. Yes, even you. It all comes down to using a few tried-and-true techniques. In this post, I’ll walk you through 10 methods that will instantly elevate your intros."

5. Pain Point → Promise → Solution

  • Identify a common pain point or problem the reader faces.

  • Offer a solution or outcome that addresses that pain point.

  • Give a high-level overview of the solution you’ll provide.

Example:

"Stuck writing intros that no one reads? You’re not alone. But with a few quick fixes, you can turn that around. In this post, I’ll share the steps you can take to write intros that pull readers in and keep them hooked."

6. Statement → Counterpoint → Thesis

  • Present a commonly held belief or assumption.

  • Challenge that belief with a counterpoint.

  • Introduce your main argument or solution.

Example:

"Most people think the secret to writing is creativity. But what if I told you that structure is far more important? In this post, I’ll show you how using structured approaches leads to better, more consistent writing."

7. Problem → Why it Matters → Preview

  • Identify a pressing problem that resonates with your reader.

  • Explain the significance of solving this problem.

  • Give a quick summary of how you will address the issue.

Example:

"Writer’s block isn’t just frustrating—it’s a productivity killer. But it doesn’t have to be that way. I’ll show you how to overcome it with simple frameworks that make writing feel effortless."

8. Challenge → Stakes → Promise

  • Present a challenge the reader is facing or may face.

  • Explain the consequences or stakes of not solving the challenge.

  • Make a promise about how the content will help solve it.

Example:

"Starting a new writing project can feel overwhelming, right? If you don’t have the right approach, it’s easy to give up before you even start. But by the end of this post, you’ll have the tools to confidently kick off any project."

9. Contradiction → Explanation → Solution

  • Introduce a surprising contradiction or unexpected insight.

  • Explain the contradiction and why it’s important.

  • Introduce your solution or the main topic of the piece.

Example:

"You might think the best writing comes from long hours of brainstorming. But the truth is, structured prompts can make your writing faster and better. I’ll show you how using a system can actually unlock creativity."

10. Hypothesis → Setup → Test

  • Propose a hypothesis that the reader can relate to.

  • Set up why this hypothesis matters and how you’ll explore it.

  • Outline how the content will test or explore this hypothesis.

Example:

"What if you could write a full blog post in under an hour? Sounds crazy, right? In this post, I’ll test that theory and show you how structured prompts can help you do just that."

How To Choose An Intro Recipe For Your Newsletter

The truth is, you can use any of these structures for any type of content—not just newsletters.

Consider what will connect best with your reader.

For example:

  • For a practical how-to guide, try a Bold Claim or Shocking Fact intro.

  • For something personal or reflective, go with the Anecdote structure.

  • For content that tackles a challenge, use the Problem → Promise → Solution format.

The key is to match your intro structure to your audience expectations.

Which is why it's so important to know who you're writing to first. So, whether you're writing for your son, burned out freelancers, or time-strapped founders, tailoring your intro to their needs will keep them reading.

Now that you have an handful of intro recipes, let's look at how you can write them with AI.

The Magnetic Intro Prompt

This prompt turns a headline into an introduction, setting the right tone and grabbing attention in just a few sentences.

It does this by using any of the 10 structured intro formulas designed to hook readers immediately.

Here's the prompt template:

Write an introduction to an newsletter about {Headline}

Adapt all your responses to this writing style & tone:

Hey there {Insert Your Target Reader}!

{Insert your intro recipe}

Direct, uses personal anecdotes.
Short to medium sentences, contractions, everyday metaphors, first-person perspective addressing the reader as 'you'.
Write in short paragraphs, often just one sentence long for emphasis.
Use line breaks between paragraphs for easy reading.

Here’s how to give the prompt a try:

  1. Start with a headline. (If you need inspiration, use the Endless Idea Generator.)

  2. Paste the prompt into an LLM like Claude or ChatGPT.

  3. Add your target audience, headline, and one of the 10 intro recipes into the prompt.

  4. Run the prompt to generate an introduction.

  5. Try different recipes until you find the one that fits best.

Let’s say your headline is “Write A Personalized Cover Letter In <2 Minutes.” You could try the Question → Problem → Solution intro recipe to address the problem of writing hundreds of personalized cover letters.

And if you're unhappy with the first result, just swap in a different recipe (or come up with your own), tweak the tone, and run it again until you land on the perfect introduction.

With these 10 intro recipes in your toolkit, you’ll have no trouble creating intros that pull readers in and keep them engaged from the first line.
