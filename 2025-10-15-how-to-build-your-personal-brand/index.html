    Back in 2014, I made an observation that changed everything about how I built my career as a writer.

    I am a huge rap and hip-hop fan, and I noticed how artists obsessed over their image. Record labels and independent musicians were pouring resources into professional photos, music videos, and carefully curated social media. Even if you’d never heard their music, one scroll through their Instagram told you: this person is a star.

    This is Branding 101.

    But when I tried to apply that to writing, I hit a brick wall.

    It didn’t exist.

    “Writers write. They don’t do social media.”

    All my favorite authors were basically invisible online.

    They publish incredible works of art and literature, but then their Instagram account consists of random photos of their cat every seven months, or a picture of what book they’re reading on a vacation from three years ago. Unless a writer used Medium, Quora, or their own site to share their thoughts, they didn’t really exist anywhere else.

    Nearly everyone I pointed this out to said:

    “That makes sense. Writers write. They don’t do social media.”

    But as a 23-year-old with zero resources and determined to build myself into a successful, fully independent writer, I saw this as a massive opportunity.

    [

    ](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!BoID!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5151913f-bf5d-43dc-a5d9-32e218011247_500x500.png)

    Kendrick Lamar

    I decided to take the branding playbook of an up-and-coming rap artist and apply it to myself.

    • If rappers hired photographers to take on tour with them and capture candid photos of them behind the scenes, then I was going to do the same thing in my everyday life.

    • If rappers made their Instagram pages give the impression that they were this massive superstar, then I was going to do the same thing.

    • If rappers had creative directors, then I was going to be my own creative director.

    The only problem was, I couldn’t afford a super-expensive photographer.

    How I Built My Personal Brand Image As A Writer

    Columbia College Chicago had a website where students could collaborate on projects.

    I’d been out of school for a year but still had access. I searched for photography students. What I found: they desperately needed models for class assignments. “Looking for a model for a nighttime shoot.” “Looking for model for experimental portrait.” They needed me as much as I needed them.

    I emailed 20 students. A few responded. And I just started showing up.

    I was terrible at modeling.

    “Look at me like you’re thinking about something,” one photographer said.

    I thought I was doing it.

    “No, like... actually move your face. Make a different expression.”

    I had no idea what she meant.

    “No, okay, so like, uhm. You know what, never mind.”

    I collaborated with probably ten different photographers in 2014. Most pictures never saw the light of day. I was really, really bad. But every once in awhile, one turned out amazing—usually by accident, from afar, looking almost candid.

    I decided that documentary look was going to be my style.

    From 2014 to 2017, this is how I populated my Instagram page (which now has more than 85,000 followers, despite the fact that it’s not really my primary platform).

    And that’s how I started to build my image as a writer online.

    [

    ](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!obS4!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7f5dfe97-d097-4da3-81e0-3e20e75506c2_1238x1540.png)

    Now I have the resources to invest more deliberately in this part of my personal brand (which I firmly, firmly believe is one of the best investments you could possibly make in yourself). When I moved to LA, I started collaborating with a very talented photographer named Walid Azami, who has photographed everyone from Usher to JLO to Kanye West.

    When people comment on my work today, one of the first things they bring up is the fact that, unlike most other writers, I am extremely visible. I attach pictures to nearly everything I write and do. As a result, I appear as more than just a writer — which was my entire goal from the beginning.

    “But I don’t want to take selfies!”

    You don’t have to.

    You can build an audience without ever posting a single photo of yourself.

    However…your readers don’t just follow your content. They follow YOU. ****Including personal photos is a simple way to make your writing feel more human—and your brand more memorable. Images help your readers feel like they know you (even though you’ve never met). Which means you become someone they recognize, remember, and ultimately trust.

    So if you want more engagement from your audience, stop hiding behind your content.

    Let people see you.

    The good news is, you don’t need a camera, a photographer, or even a photoshoot to do it.

    How To Build An Engaging Personal Brand Photo Library For Social Media With Nano Banana

    Google’s new Nano Banana (part of Gemini) lets you upload one good photo and remix it into countless new ones.

    You can change the setting, outfit, hairstyle — even the decade — all with words.

    • Need a photo for a post about productivity? Put yourself in a coffee shop.

    • Writing about travel? Drop yourself into that destination.

    • Need variety? Generate ten versions of the same look.

    Back in 2014, no writer was doing this—and it helped me stand out.

    It still works.

    And you can start doing it today.

    For free.

    Here’s the step-by-step playbook to generate a professional-looking brand photo library in minutes:

    Step 1: Secure Your “Anchor Photo”

    To build a consistent brand, you need one, single, high-quality image that Nano Banana will use as your “Anchor.”

    This is the photo the AI will reference to keep your face, build, and overall likeness consistent. Choose a headshot or a half-body shot where you are well-lit, looking directly at the camera, and wearing a neutral or simple outfit.

    A basic selfie or a friend’s photo works perfectly.

    Example:

    Step 2: Choose A Target Scene / Photo

    Pull up Google image search.

    Then use your imagination.

    If you’ve always wanted to travel to Paris, search for “selfies in Paris.”

    Here’s one I found:

    [

    ](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!1P1R!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8e53f1ed-18d7-4c96-9c7e-76cd4f9b37b6_686x386.png)

    The sky is the limit.

    • Woman walking in Central Park, NYC.

    • Man sitting sipping an espresso in an Italian bistro with a dog.

    You get the idea.

    Step 3: Generate An AI Image Recreation Prompt For Your Photo Using ChatGPT

    Once you have your target scene, the next step is to generate a scene prompt.

    This prompt takes any reference image and generates a new prompt you can use to create the exact same scene. The output is a detailed blueprint of any photo you upload.

    Here’s the prompt:

    # AI IMAGE RECREATION PROMPT

You are a professional visual reference analyst and technical art director. 

Analyze the uploaded image and produce a scene recreation prompt so that an AI image generator can rebuild it identically. 

Do not add style, mood, or cinematic interpretation. 

Your role is to measure:

## 1. SUBJECT PLACEMENT & BODY ORIENTATION
## 2. SECONDARY FIGURES & RELATIONSHIPS
## 3. FOREGROUND ELEMENTS
## 4. MIDGROUND COMPOSITION
## 5. BACKGROUND ENVIRONMENT
## 6. CAMERA ANGLE, FRAMING & DEPTH OF FIELD
## 7. LIGHTING CONDITIONS
## 8. COLOR PALETTE & TONAL RANGE
## 9. OVERALL VISUAL ATMOSPHERE
## 10. FINAL DIRECTIVE
> “Recreate this exact scene, maintaining all environmental elements, composition, lighting, and gestures identical, replacing only the main subject with the provided reference character’s likeness.”

Be technical, specific, and factual.

---

    I recommend you use ChatGPT to generate the image recreation prompt.

    While Gemini can generate image prompts too, ChatGPT is stronger at analyzing visual structure and producing highly detailed, instruction-level descriptions. It’s essentially doing the heavy lifting of “scene direction” for you.

    Step 4: The “Keep Me Consistent” Nano Banana Prompt

    Now you have everything you need to create your photo.

    All you have to do is tell Nano Banana to insert YOU into the scene.

    Here’s how:

    1. Upload your Anchor Photo to Gemini

    2. Select Tools —> Create Images

    3. Copy/paste the below prompt

    4. Insert the Scene prompt the last step

    5. Run it!

    For example:

    Bananas, right?

    If you didn’t notice the small Gemini logo in the bottom corner, you’d assume I actually took this photo.

    Here’s the prompt:

    Analyze the subject in the attached image.

Generate an image of them into the scene described in this prompt. 

Only the identity must transfer. 

The generated result must be unmistakably the same person, with no substitutions or lookalikes. 

Keep all environmental and lighting conditions locked. 

- Use the same camera position, lens type, and sunlight direction as described.  
- Lock all environmental elements (terrain, background, crowd, lighting) as fixed constants.  
- Only modify the main subject’s identity, preserving all other visual variables.  
- Ensure natural skin tone matching, realistic shadow casting, and seamless compositing.

# SCENE

{Insert The Scene Prompt You Created Using Your Reference Image}

    This prompt trains the AI to add your likeness into your selected scene.

    Faces stay accurate because you’re not asking the model to re-pose or re-interpret identity—only to restyle the same pixels.

    A mistake people make when using Nano Banana is they upload two photos and then ask Gemini to “insert me into the photo.” This confuses the AI and you’ll end up with a photo that looks more like a JibJab eCard elf dance instead of a photorealistic version of you.

    [

    ](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mivR!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe67d6b28-5f9f-4651-9a02-52514db4a7ae_1024x640.png)

    Another thing to watch out for is AI “look and feel.”

    AI image generators don’t actually know what a “photo” is in the physical sense — they know patterns of pixels associated with the idea of photography. So when you give them a detailed description, they interpret it as a concept to visualize, not a camera-captured moment to reproduce.

    That’s why, even with precise spatial detail, the output can feel like a painting or 3D render. The model defaults to its most statistically “safe” representation of realism which often leans slightly idealized or stylized unless you constrain it—which is what you’re going to do in the next step.

    Step 5: Render Photos For Different Camera Styles

    Once the base image is clean, you can render the image for different styles.

    Below is a collection of “render as…” clauses you can append to any technical scene description to lock the output into the right visual domain. You can treat these like photo presets. Just pick the one that matches the source image type, paste it at the end of your descriptive prompt in Step 4, and the model will stay anchored in that photographic realism instead of drifting toward AI stylization.

    Here are 5 you can try:

    1. Standard Digital Photo

    Render as an unedited digital photograph captured with a professional full-frame DSLR camera using a 50 mm lens at f/8, ISO 200, daylight white balance. Maintain true-to-life textures, natural shadows, and realistic human skin tones. No stylization, no illustration effects, no cinematic filters.

    2. Classic 35 mm Film Photo

    Render as a color photograph captured on 35 mm Kodak Portra 400 film using a 50 mm lens at f/5.6. Preserve authentic film grain, soft contrast, and natural light falloff. No HDR or oversharpening.

    3. Vintage Black-and-White Film

    Render as a monochrome photograph captured on 35 mm Ilford HP5 Plus film with visible film grain and soft mid-tones. Maintain natural contrast, subtle halation, and authentic analog texture.

    4. News / Documentary Photo

    Render as an authentic photojournalistic image taken with a handheld DSLR using a 35 mm lens at f/4, available light only. Keep motion blur, natural imperfections, and realistic color balance typical of reportage photography. No stylization or cinematic tone mapping

    5. Polaroid / Instant Film

    Render as an unedited Polaroid instant photo with slight vignetting, imperfect focus, and natural color shifts. Preserve authentic film borders, uneven exposure, and soft highlights.

    Each preset changes the entire mood of your photo while keeping your likeness consistent.

    Step 6: Stock Up Your AI Photo Library

    Your personal brand needs range.

    So, create photos for different types of content:

    • Professional settings (office, coffee shop, workspace)

    • Lifestyle contexts (walking outdoors, reading, traveling)

    • Industry-specific environments (conferences, studios, labs)

    As you write, you’ll notice certain topics come up repeatedly.

    Create dedicated photos for these themes. Then organize your generated photos in folders by theme, setting, or mood. This makes it effortless to match the right image to your content without re-generating every time you publish.

    The beauty of this system is you can create 50+ professional brand photos in an afternoon—something that would have cost thousands of dollars and weeks of scheduling back in 2014.

    The question isn’t whether you can build a personal brand image as a writer anymore.

    The question is: will you?

    That’s it.
