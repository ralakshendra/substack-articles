    Ahoy, Digital Writers!

    Last week we explored how to build a step-by-step guide with iterative prompts.

    I showed you how I created a guide for the “What If” Storytelling Framework. But I didn’t give you the completed framework. Which is what I’m sharing today.

    So the next time you write an email, need a quick story for your article, or want to practice storytelling, you’ll have this dead-simple framework (and the prompts) to get you going.

    Here’s the thing:

    Stories connect us. And you know this. But finding a story to tell is tough.

    It’s easy to fall back on worn out boring anecdotes.

    Fortunately, there's an easy fix.

    Learn this one skill and you’ll be a top 1% storyteller.

    The skill?

    Observe the world and ask "What if?"

    This simple question unlocks creativity. It pushes you to challenge assumptions and imagine alternate realities. Rather than a blank page, "What if" gives your brain a launching point. You take your reader from "what is" to "could be."

    And that's the essence of storytelling—taking the mundane and making it marvelous.

    And with the 5-Step “What If” Storytelling Framework, you'll never lack stories again.

    Let’s break it down.

    Step 1: What Do You See?

    The first step in the What If Storytelling Framework is to notice something around you.

    It could be anything—a tree, a phone, a book. Look around you and pick 1 thing. For example, let’s say you observe a busy ant hill in the park.

    Write down “ants” and move to the next step.

    Step 2: What’s True?

    After you have your object, the next think you need to do is write down a factual statement about your observation.

    For example:

    • “The ants are red.”

    • "The ants are working together to build their hill."

    • “The ant hill has a footprint on it (and it looks like mine).”

    You’re rockin’ and rollin.’

    Let’s keep going.

    Step 3: Ask "What If"

    Once you have your observation and a list of “truisms” you can start to brainstorm some What If questions to help you formulate a story idea.

    I’ve included 6 to get you thinking.

    What if [opposite of what you observed]?

    In other words, flip the truth.

    All you have to do is invert a truth you identified about your observation. The reason this works is because is helps you to explore how changing a fundamental aspect can offer new perspectives. So, think about the most obvious fact of your observation and simply reverse it.

    For example:

    • "What if water flowed uphill?"

    • "What if the sky was green instead of blue?"

    • "What if the ants were all working alone instead of together?"

    What if [your observation] was used for [a different purpose]?

    Consider what you observe is least likely to be used for, and use that.

    Assign a new or opposite purpose. Thinking this way stimulates creativity. It forces you to break common conventions which makes for a great surprise in a story.

    For example:

    • "What if school buses were used as greenhouses?"

    • "What if smartphones were solely for educational purposes?"

    • "What if the ant hill was a meeting spot for other insects to trade food?"

    What if [your observation] could [new ability]?

    Think of a superpower (real or magical).

    • Change the color

    • Hide in plain sight

    • Move at hyper-speed

    Then push the boundaries of your observation.

    For example:

    • "What if cars could repair themselves?"

    • "What if trees could move from place to place?"

    • "What if the ants could communicate with humans?"

    What if [your observation] was [much larger/smaller]?

    Dramatically change the size of what you observed.

    Imagine your observation at a scale. Or shrink it down to nothing. This will help you see how size influences function and perception.

    For example:

    • "What if the ant hill was as big as a house?"

    • "What if a cat was as large as an elephant?"

    • "What if a smartphone was the size of a postage stamp?"

    What if [your observation] existed in [different time period]?

    Context affects our understanding and use of objects—which makes for great storytelling material.

    So, place your observation in a different time period. Pick a time period that contrasts sharply with the present day in terms of technology, culture, or environment.

    For example:

    • "What if ants had the technology of humans in the medieval times?"

    • "What if dinosaurs roamed the earth today?"

    • "What if smartphones existed in Ancient Egypt?"

    What if [your observation] was like [fictional element]?

    Combine your observation with an element from your favorite book, movie, or game.

    • The Force from Start Wars

    • The Sorting Hat from Harry Potter

    • The Oasis from Ready Player One

    By blending the boundaries between reality and fiction, you’ll push the imaginative potential of your observation.

    For example:

    • "What if ants organized their societies like the kingdoms in fantasy novels?"

    • "What if the trees in the forest communicated like the Ents in 'The Lord of the Rings'?"

    • "What if cars were sentient beings like in 'Cars'?"

    Train ChatGPT To Ask What If Questions

    You can work through these ideas on your own or you can use ChatGPT to speed up the process by using the following prompt.

    I am going to train you to use the "What If" question technique.

Here's how it works:

You will ask me for an object. For example, paint, cup, pencil, etc.

I will tell you what kind of object I want to focus on.

Then you are going to give me a list of 6 "what if" questions that I can use as a prompt for telling a story.

These questions are going to help me tell a non-fiction story—like an anecdote. 

I'm a beginner. So your output needs to be clear for clear for 13 year old.

For example, if I observe paint on a wall. 

Then you might say, "what if paint could talk?" 

Here are 6 "what if" categories of questions to consider:
1. Flip the truth. For example, "What if the ants were all working alone instead of together?"
2. Change a purpose. For example,"What if the ant hill was a meeting spot for other insects to trade food?"
3. Add a new ability. For example, "What if the ants could communicate with humans?"
4. Alter the size. For example,"What if the ant hill was as big as a house?"
5. Shift the time. For example, "What if ants had the technology of humans in the medieval times?"
6. Mix with fiction. For example, "What if ants organized their societies like the kingdoms in fantasy novels?"

Do you understand?

    Review the list of questions and choose one that sparks your interest.

    Then move to Step 4.

    Step 4: Turn Your “What If” Into A 5-Sentence Story

    Now you are ready to answer the What If question and build out your story.

    To do this, we are going to follow this simple 5-sentence story format.

    It works like this:

    • Sentence 1: Set the scene with time, person, and place.

    • Sentence 2: Introduce a conflict or problem.

    • Sentence 3: Describe the attempted solution.

    • Sentence 4: Share the result of that attempt.

    • Sentence 5: End with a lesson that applies to your target audience

    Pretty simple.

    Sticking with our ant story from above, here’s how it breaks down.

    • Scene: "Last summer, Andy the ant was alone in a big, busy garden."

    • Problem: "He found out doing stuff like getting food and making a home was really tough on his own."

    • Solution: "Andy then had a bright idea to team up with other ants for the tough tasks."

    • Result: "Working together, they did awesome stuff, way more than Andy ever did by himself."

    • Lesson: "So, if you're trying to do everything in your business by yourself, remember Andy the ant. Working with others can help you do better than going it alone."

    Just like that, you've got a story!

    And here’s a prompt if you’re stuck.

    I want you to write a 5-sentence story for my TARGET AUDIENCE given a WHAT IF QUESTION.

WHAT IF QUESTION = {Your Question}

TARGET AUDIENCE = {Your Audience}

Follow this 5-structure story structure:

- Sentence 1: Set the scene with time, person, and place.
- Sentence 2: Introduce a conflict or problem.
- Sentence 3: Describe the attempted solution.
- Sentence 4: Share the result of that attempt.
- Sentence 5: End with a lesson for my TARGET AUDIENCE

Write using simple short sentences. 

7th grade reading  level. 

Get to the point.

    Now it’s time to tell it.

    Step 5: Tell Your Story

    Share your story with a friend, family member, or post it on social media.

    Make note of how your audience reacts. Gather data about what works and what doesn't work. Look for signal. Refine what works.

    Re-tell the stories that resonate.

    And keep building your library of stories.

    Remember, the best stories are the ones you have fun telling.

    That’s it!
