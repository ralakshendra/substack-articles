What Is B-Roll?

B-roll is extra video or images that help tell your story.

Think of it like adding color to a black-and-white sketch. It’s how you paint emotion, emphasis, and clarity into the picture. The visuals reinforce what you’re already saying in your script.

If your viewer hears “Here's how I organize my day,” and sees a quick animation of a calendar getting filled in, that’s b-roll. If they hear you say “I hit a wall after writing 3 scripts in a row,” and see a photo of someone rubbing their eyes at a laptop, that’s b-roll.

And it doesn’t always have to be video.

B-roll can be:

  • A photo

  • A chart or infographic

  • A looping animation

  • A drawing

  • Even subtle text or visuals that play behind your voice

If it helps the viewer see what you’re saying, it counts.

Why B-Roll Matters (Even In Short Videos)

In writing, you use bold text, bullet points, or formats like 1 / 3 / 1 to keep your reader engaged.

In video, you use visuals.

B-roll is how you hold attention. It breaks up the monotony of a talking head. It reinforces what you're saying. It hides jump cuts, smooths transitions, and—most importantly—it keeps people watching.

And you don’t need a lot.

Just 1 to 3 well-timed visuals can make a huge difference in how polished your video feels.

As a general rule, aim to show something new every 3 to 5 seconds—even if it’s subtle. That’s why almost every high-performing Short you see on TikTok, Instagram, or YouTube has captions, cutaways, or quick visuals baked in.

Now—how you use b-roll depends on the format of your Short:

  • Talking Head: B-roll cuts away from your face so viewers aren’t staring at you the entire time. It adds variety and helps you visually explain what you're saying.

  • Faceless: B-roll is the video. There’s no anchor visual, so your b-roll clips need to do 100% of the storytelling work.

  • AI Avatar: You’ve got a “host” on screen, but the b-roll still plays the same role—it supports your voiceover and gives the viewer something fresh to look at every few seconds.

Next, I’ll show you how to actually use b-roll—and how to create it from scratch using AI tools like Sora.

7 Go-To Types Of B-Roll For Shorts

Here are 7 go-to types I recommend for beginners, especially if you’re creating educational content.

1. Process B-Roll

Let’s start with the most basic kind: process shots.

These are simple visuals that show the action you're talking about—typing on a laptop, writing in a journal, sketching on a whiteboard. They work so well because they match your words literally.

Process b-roll is perfect for for how-to content, breakdowns, or walkthroughs. If you're teaching a 3-step method, show the viewer what that process looks like in motion.

One short clip per step is often enough.

2. Relatable Struggle

When you describe a common pain point—like feeling overwhelmed, stuck, or distracted—you can pair it with a visual of someone rubbing their face, drowning in tabs, or slouching at their desk.

The reasons these work is because people see themselves in it. It triggers a “yep, that’s me” moment.

You can also keep it super simple.

In this Short, for example, notice how I use emojis that parallel what I’m saying in the script. It's subtle, but it helps the viewer stay emotionally connected to the message.

Even basic images or overlays can add depth.

3. Transformation / Result

This is your “after” shot. It’s what you want the viewer to aspire to.

Weight loss is an easy example here. But it could be a clean workspace, a happy client, inbox zero, someone calmly checking off a task list.

It’s useful because it visually reinforces the point of your video. You're not just giving advice—you’re showing what life looks like after that advice is applied.

4. Tools, Numbers, & Data

If you talk about calendars, apps, dashboards, or Notion templates… don’t just mention them—show them.

“Tool” b-roll adds specificity. It grounds your advice in reality and helps visual learners see how it works. Same for data. Stats, revenue charts, dashboards, timelines—this kind of b-roll signals that you're not just giving opinions. You're backing it up.

You can screen record your actual tools and metrics, or use AI prompts to create “fake but familiar” versions that still do the job.

Just be sure there’s some motion: a cursor dragging, a window opening, a calendar updating. Static screenshots don’t hold attention.

5. Abstract Concepts

This is where you use metaphor. Instead of literally showing what you're talking about, you represent the idea behind it.

Examples:

  • A match lighting in darkness = inspiration

  • A maze = confusion

  • A plant growing = progress

  • A well-known character = what they stand for

It’s powerful because it adds mood and meaning—especially when you’re talking about mindset shifts, deeper lessons, or non-tactical takeaways.

For example, in this Reel Dickie uses a short clip of Frank Abagnale to illustrate the concept of “faking it” as an authority.

Just keep it on-brand with your tone. If your vibe is playful, don’t drop in a dark, brooding shot of a rainy window.

6. Daily Life Cutaways

This is where you give your content some texture.

Think:

  • Brewing coffee

  • Walking through the park

  • Closing your laptop after a long workday.

Use this b-roll to make your videos feel more human.

Great for story-based content or lifestyle-flavored teaching. Just be sure the vibe matches the tone of your script—don’t pair peaceful b-roll with high-energy narration.

7. “What Not To Do” Visuals

Sometimes, the fastest way to teach something is to show what not to do.

This is contrast-based b-roll.

It lets you highlight a common mistake or bad habit—and makes your advice feel more useful by comparison.

Use it right before you offer a fix. That way, the viewer feels the problem visually before you give them the solution.

Where To Get B-Roll (And How to Make Your Own)

Once you know what kind of b-roll you need, the next question is:

“Where do I find it—or do I have to shoot it all myself?”

Good news: You have options.

Option 1: Use AI Tools To Generate It

You can use Sora or ChatGPT to can generate high-quality b-roll from a simple text prompt.

You don’t need a camera, lighting, or a studio. Just describe what you want to see—and the tool creates it for you This is powerful because you can create b-roll that perfectly matches your script.

Option 2: Use Stock B-Roll Libraries

If you want plug-and-play footage and don’t need custom visuals, stock libraries are a great option.

Most editing tools like CapCut give you access to stock and b-roll footage. So you don’t need to cobble together multiple tools to make this work.

Option 3: Shoot Your Own

Yes, you can absolutely make your own b-roll—even if all you have is a phone.

And in some cases, you should.

Especially when you want to show your actual workflow, space, or face.

A few tips here:

  • Use natural light (shoot near a window)

  • Keep it steady (use a tripod, stack of books, or even your coffee mug)

  • Film simple, repeatable tasks (typing, writing, clicking, walking)

Even 2–3 seconds of custom footage can make your video feel more you.

Which Should You Use?

There’s no one right answer.

But here’s a simple way to decide:

  • Use AI b-roll when you want control + speed

  • Use stock b-roll when you want convenience

  • Shoot your own b-roll when you want authenticity

If you’re just starting out, try AI + stock for your first few videos. Then, layer in custom b-roll once you find your rhythm.

Here are the tools I recommend for sourcing (or creating) your visuals:

  • Sora/ChatGPT: Use for cinematic AI-generated shots that look original.

  • CapCut: The fastest way to add stock footage and simple AI effects in your edit

  • Canva: Perfect for static visuals, mockups, headlines, slides, diagrams

  • Your Phone: Seriously, film your desk, hands, workspace—it makes it personal

  • Loom/Tella: Ideal for screen recordings, walkthroughs, or product demos

Most creators cycle between just 2–3 of these tools.

So what makes a good video prompt?

It’s simple.

You just need to think like a camera operator. Can you see the moment in your head? Could you shoot it with your phone?

If yes, you’re on the right track.

AI is trying to film a scene based on exactly what you told it.

Watch out for these video prompting mistakes

If your prompt isn’t something you could shoot with a camera, it’s probably going to miss the mark.

Let me walk you through three common prompt problems—and how to fix them.

1. Too conceptual

For example: “Show burnout.”

That’s a feeling. There’s no action. No setting. No framing. Just a mood.

Instead, describe something you could actually point a camera at. Something like: “A person slumped over their desk at night, dark lighting, cluttered workspace.” Now the AI has a place, a subject, and something to film.

You’ve turned a concept into a scene.

2. Too vague

For example: “Someone using technology in a modern space.”

What does that even mean? Is it a laptop? VR headset? A phone? Are they in a WeWork? A spaceship?

Instead, get specific.

Try something like: “Close-up of hands typing on a Mac laptop at a Starbuck’s cafe, green neon sign in the background.”

Now the AI knows what device, what action, what setting, and even the lighting vibe.

Way more useful.

3. Too much happening

This one happens when people try to cram an entire sequence into a single prompt:

“Someone wakes up, gets coffee, walks to their desk, and starts working.”

That’s four shots. The AI’s going to average them all into something dull and generic.

Here’s how to fix it:

Break it down into simple, individual moments.

  • “Coffee being poured into a mug in slow motion.”

  • “Overhead shot of a laptop opening on a desk.”

One shot = one prompt.
