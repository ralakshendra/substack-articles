Did you see this?

Last week, Anthropic shipped a feature called Computer Use.

  • Opens your apps

  • Navigates your browser

  • Updates your docs and trackers

Anything you’d normally do sitting at your desk, Claude can now do on its own.

There’s a whole new category of work you couldn't delegate to AI—until now.

Before Computer Use, here’s how every AI workflow worked:

  1. You go get some data (login, click, scroll, export)

  2. You paste it into Claude

  3. You ask Claude to write, analyze, or summarize

  4. You take the output and put it somewhere else

Steps 1 and 4 were always on you.

You were the middleman between the AI and your apps. Now Claude does all four steps. It opens your app. It pulls the data. It does the analysis. And it puts the output where you need it.

Said differently:

Any workflow where you’re manually pulling data from one place and doing something with it in another place is now one prompt.

If that sounds abstract, let me show you what this looks like

Subscribed

Example: Audit Your Substack Notes Library With “Computer Use”

Write With AI gets 150+ subscribes every month from Substack Notes.

But if you want to understand what Notes drive growth, it takes 5+ minutes of clicking just to see the data.

  • Login to Substack

  • Click into Growth

  • Click on New Subscribers

  • Filter by timeframe and category

  • Scroll down to Notes

Now, once you get here you can see what posts are resonating with your readers.

[

But you're still just looking at a list of numbers with no context.

The hard part is doing something with that data once you have it.

  • Categorizing Notes by topic.

  • Spotting which categories are trending up week over week.

  • Connecting the patterns to what you should write next.

Which is exactly the kind of work Computer Use was built for.

So I asked Claude to do all three in one prompt👇

Go to the writewithai.substack.com dashboard.

  • Navigate to Growth → New Subscribers.

  • Filter to 7 days.

  • Expand the Substack source to see Notes.

  • Pull every Note that drove a subscriber this week.

Create a spreadsheet so I can track the data, including each Note, its subscriber count, and its topic category. Then analyze the patterns and suggest 5 new topic ideas I can write about based on what's working.

Schedule this to repeat every Sunday at 9am and notify me when it's done.

Here’s what happened:

  • Step 1: Claude asked for permission. Claude showed me its plan and asked to access Chrome. Nothing happens without you approving each app. (This is important — you’re always in control.)

  • Step 2: Claude navigated to my Substack dashboard. Claude opened Chrome, went to Substack, and clicked into the Growth tab. Then it filtered to “New Subscribers” and selected the last 7 days.

But I would never have noticed them by glancing at my dashboard once a month. The value is in the consistency. Which is why next, I asked Claude set this up to run every Sunday at 9am.

If you’re not familiar, Claude also has a built-in scheduling feature inside Cowork. You can set any task to repeat hourly, daily, weekly, or on weekdays.

Setting up a schedule takes about 30 seconds. You can either type “/schedule” inside any Cowork task and Claude will walk you through it, or click “Scheduled” in the left sidebar and hit “+ New task” to fill in the details yourself (task name, instructions, frequency, and which folder to save the output to).

Note: Scheduled tasks only run while your Mac is awake and Claude Desktop is open. If your computer is asleep when a task is supposed to fire, Claude skips it and reruns it automatically once your Mac wakes back up. You get a notification either way, so you always know what happened.

How To Set Up Claude Computer Use

To get started, you need two things:

  1. Claude Pro or Max plan ($20/month or $100/month)

  2. Claude Desktop app Download from claude.ai/download

When you’re ready, open the Claude desktop app.

In the top, you’ll see a toggle that says “Cowork.” Click it. This is the mode that gives Claude access to your computer. It’s separate from the normal chat window.

Then complete the following steps

Step 1: Turn On Computer Use

Inside Cowork, Claude can already read and write files.

But to have it actually navigate your browser and click through your Substack dashboard, you need Computer Use enabled.

Go to Claude desktop app → Settings → look for “Computer Use” under Features. Toggle it on. This is what gives Claude the ability to see your screen, move your mouse, and interact with apps the way you would.

[

](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!IPcd!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa65b84f4-9af1-41c5-bb1f-37de06d9d91c_2394x916.png)

Step 2: Give Cowork Your Task

Paste the prompt from above into the chat (or customize it for your own Substack).

Claude will show you its plan before it starts, and you can review each step. The first time it needs to interact with an app (like Chrome or Finder), it will pause and ask your permission. You approve each app individually.

Easy.

And now that you’re setup:

Try These 5 Computer Use Prompts

The real power of Computer Use shows up when Claude starts jumping between apps on your computer.

Give these 5 a try

1. Content Template Mining From LinkedIn

Visit these 3 LinkedIn profiles: [URL 1], [URL 2], [URL 3]

For each creator, scroll through their recent posts and find their 3 highest-engagement posts (most likes and comments). Read each post.

For every post, break down the structural pattern: the hook (first 2 lines), the format (list, story, framework, contrarian take?), the body structure, and where the CTA lands.

Create a spreadsheet logging all 15 posts with their engagement numbers, hook type, format type, and a one-line summary of why it works.

Then create a separate document with 5 reusable post templates with placeholder sections I can fill in with my own topic.

[

](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!IN9T!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F86adba42-a66e-45be-b6b2-64109e28f8f6_2948x1310.png)

2. Weekly Business Dashboard

Check these sources and build me a weekly dashboard:

  • Substack: total subscribers, new subscribers this week, churn, and top post performance

  • Stripe: total revenue this week, number of new customers, and MRR

  • [Social platform]: follower count and top-performing post this week

Create a spreadsheet with a tab for each source.

Add a “Dashboard” tab that shows the 8-10 numbers that matter most, this week vs. last week, with % change.

At the bottom, write 3 bullet points: what’s working, what’s stalling, and one thing to focus on next week.

3. Weekly Substack Performance Report

Your growth data lives across multiple dashboards behind multiple logins. Substack for subscribers. Stripe for revenue. Google Analytics for traffic. Nobody consolidates it — because it takes 45 minutes of tab-switching every Monday morning.

Go to my Substack dashboard. Pull this week’s subscriber count, open rate for the most recent post, and top subscriber sources.

Go to my Stripe dashboard. Pull this week’s total revenue and number of transactions.

Go to Google Analytics for [your site]. Pull this week’s page views, top traffic sources, and top-performing pages.

Create a single spreadsheet with a tab for each source. Add a “Summary” tab that shows all key metrics side by side with last week’s numbers and the % change for each.

Highlight anything that changed by more than 15%.

[

](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!36RH!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F75561156-0c5b-4b7a-a887-313a5cebd3f8_2438x1564.png)

4. Competitive Newsletter Analysis

I write about [TOPIC] on Substack.

Find 5 competing newsletters.

For each one, pull: their newsletter description, posting frequency, whether they have a paid tier (and the price), their most recent 5 post titles, and the number of likes on each post.

Create a spreadsheet comparing all 5 side by side.

Then create a separate document with a one-page competitive positioning analysis:

  • What topics are they covering that I’m not?

  • What angles are they using? W

  • Where is there a white space I could own?

End with 3 specific recommendations for how I should differentiate.

5. Reader Reply Mining

Open Gmail.

Search for every reply to my last 10 newsletters (subject line contains “[newsletter name]” or is a reply to my Substack email address).

Read through the replies and extract every question readers asked (phrased or implied).

Create a spreadsheet with three columns:

  • Reader question

  • Topic category

  • Topic frequence

Sort by frequency, highest first.

Add a second tab with a prioritized list of 10 newsletter topics based on what readers are asking about most.

Include a suggested angle for each one.
